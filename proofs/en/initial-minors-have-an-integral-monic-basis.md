# Initial minors have an integral monic basis

[Proof catalogue](../../PROOFS.en.md) · [Reading and review status](../../STATUS.md)

Let $R$ be an integral domain. Work with the variables $X_{a,t}$ of a matrix having $r$ rows and $n$ columns. Fix a Young diagram $\nu$ of at most $n$ rows and write $\nu_t$ for its row lengths in descending order. For each column of $\nu$ of length $h$, choose $h$ strictly increasing row indices $I$ of the matrix and form $\det X_{I,[1,h]}$. Let $M_\nu$ be the polynomial span of the products of these minors, one minor per diagram column. Then $M_\nu$ admits a basis of such products whose leading monomials are distinct and have coefficient $1$. This gives a linear projection onto $M_\nu$ defined over $\mathbb Z$ when $R=\mathbb Z$.

Use lexicographic order with earlier matrix columns first and, within each column, smaller row indices first. The leading monomial of a minor is its diagonal, since the first unequal column between the diagonal and another determinant term uses a larger row index in the latter. It has coefficient $1$; products have the product of these leading monomials. Each product is homogeneous of column multidegree $\nu$, meaning $\nu_t$ occurrences from column $t$. It is also invariant under adding any scalar multiple of column $l$ to column $j$ for $l<j$: each affected determinant uses both columns. These conditions define the polynomial module called `dualWeyl` here.

We prove a restriction on the leading exponent vector $m$ of any nonzero polynomial $F$ in this invariant module. For two adjacent columns $l,j=l+1$, it is
$$
\sum_{b\le a}m_{b,j}\ \le\ \sum_{b<a}m_{b,l}\qquad\text{for every row }a. \tag{1}
$$
In fact, if the degree in the indeterminate $T$ after adding $T$ times column $l$ to column $j$ is at most $s$, replace the right side by $s+\sum_{b<a}m_{b,l}$. To see this, take the homogeneous component containing the leading monomial for the grading that counts the total degree in $X_{b,l},X_{b,j}$ for each row $b$ and fixes the exponents in the other columns. The shear preserves this grading, so the component still has $T$-degree at most $s$. Its leading monomial is the original one.

Process the rows from smallest to largest. In the first unprocessed row, put $x=X_{a,l}$, $y=X_{a,j}$. If the degree in this pair is $K+e$, with $K$ the largest exponent of $x$, write the component as $\sum_{k=0}^K x^k y^{K+e-k}W_k$, with $W_K\ne0$ and $W_k$ independent of $x,y$. The leading monomial uses $x^K y^e$, and its other exponents are the leading exponents of $W_K$, because earlier rows in the pair have been removed and the non-pair exponents were fixed. Write $\widetilde W_k(T)$ for the shear on the remaining rows. After the full shear the polynomial is divisible by $(y+Tx)^e$. In the integral domain the degree in $T$ of a product adds, so $e\le s$. Divide by $(y+Tx)^e$; the quotient has $T$-degree at most $s-e$ and $y$-degree at most $K$. At $x=1,\ y=-T$, only the $k=K$ term survives. Hence $\deg_T\widetilde W_K\le s-e+K$. Apply the same argument to $W_K$ with this updated degree bound. At the current row, the right exponent is at most the current bound; at every later row the update has removed $e$ and added $K$. This is precisely the cumulative inequality with the additional $s$. Invariance gives $s=0$, proving (1).

We can now realize $m$ by a product of minors. In each matrix column $t$, list its row indices in increasing order with the multiplicities $m_{a,t}$. The list has length $\nu_t$. For adjacent lists, by (1) the $k$-th entry of the earlier list is strictly smaller than the $k$-th entry of the later list whenever both are defined. Indeed, if the later entry equals $a$, its list has at least $k$ entries $\le a$, so the earlier list has at least $k$ entries $<a$. For each fixed $k$, take the $k$-th entries across the nonempty lists. These form a strictly increasing sequence of length equal to the height of column $k$ of $\nu$. They prescribe one minor. Their product has leading exponent $m$. This is the construction by removing the smallest entry in each nonempty list and iterating.

Choose one such product $P_m$ for each exponent vector satisfying (1) and the column degrees. Subtract the leading coefficient of $F$ times $P_m$ from $F$. The remainder remains invariant of the same multidegree and has strictly smaller leading monomial or is zero. The fixed degrees leave only finitely many possible monomials, so repeating expresses $F$ in the $P_m$. These polynomials are independent by their distinct monic leading terms. They also lie in $M_\nu$, so the invariant module equals that span.

To get a projection over the integers, operate on all polynomial monomials. In the fixed multidegree, replace each monomial indexed by an $m$ in the basis with $P_m$, and leave the other monomials unchanged. The change of basis is triangular with diagonal $1$ over $\mathbb Z$. Select the $P_m$-components in this basis. Monomials in the other multidegrees are sent to zero. This constructs a $\mathbb Z$-linear map fixing the span of the minors, with image that same span. It remains a projection after changing the coefficient ring, because the triangular change of basis and its inverse are integral.
